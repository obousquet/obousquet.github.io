# Grothendieck-Style Rising Sea: Examples

Use these examples to identify ways of developing a mathematical world. Each suggests a
research program of constructions, examples, foundational laws, and structural results, not
just a one-step replacement of the language of a conjecture.

## Schemes

Schemes enlarge algebraic varieties so that nilpotents, arithmetic base rings, gluing, and generic
behavior live in one category. Many statements about varieties become functorial after passing to
schemes.

Transfer lesson: if examples force boundary, infinitesimal, or arithmetic phenomena that the native
objects cannot represent, enlarge the object class until those phenomena are first-class.

Source: https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01I1

## Sheaves and cohomology

Sheaves encode local-to-global data, and sheaf cohomology measures the obstruction to assembling
local information globally.

Transfer lesson: if every local proof works but global assembly fails, stop treating assembly as
bookkeeping. Define the object whose cohomology or obstruction class measures the failure.

Source: https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/006U

## Etale cohomology and Weil conjectures

Grothendieck's development of etale cohomology supplied a cohomology theory for algebraic varieties
over finite fields with the formal properties needed to approach the Weil conjectures.

Transfer lesson: when the desired proof requires a tool that does not exist in the current category,
build the theory whose formal properties would make the proof natural, then prove comparison and
finiteness theorems.

Source: https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/03YQ

## Toposes

Topos theory abstracts sheaf categories and treats spaces through the logic and sheaves they support.
It unifies geometric and logical viewpoints.

Transfer lesson: if two domains repeatedly produce the same descent, forcing, or local-truth
phenomena, search for a common ambient category where both are instances.

Source: https://ncatlab.org/nlab/show/topos

## Motives

The theory of motives seeks a universal cohomology theory underlying many cohomology theories.

Transfer lesson: if several invariants prove parallel theorems with the same formal properties,
define the universal object that explains why those invariants are shadows of one structure.

Source: https://ncatlab.org/nlab/show/motive

## "Rising sea" strategy

The rising-sea metaphor is associated with Grothendieck's preference for immersing a hard problem in
a sufficiently general theory until direct resistance disappears.

Transfer lesson: study the natural theory of the objects broadly and deeply enough that the
initial problem can acquire a new place within it. Build foundations, generalizations, and
connections whose eventual interaction may be unforeseeable. The aim is for the conjecture to
become a natural consequence; it need not literally become a tautology.

Source: https://mathoverflow.net/questions/408811/grothendiecks-rising-sea-approach

## How to use these examples

For a live problem, sketch the surrounding mathematical territory:

```text
What operations keep appearing?
Which operations are currently ad hoc?
What data is invisible in the native objects?
What local-to-global failure keeps recurring?
What invariant has the same formal behavior across examples?
What larger classes and constructions deserve study?
Which neighboring theories illuminate these objects?
What questions arise naturally even if the initiating conjecture is set aside?
What would a coherent account of this territory still need to explain?
```

These questions help organize a developing theory. They do not require choosing one master
theorem or identifying a return to the original conjecture before substantial work begins.

## A Theory-Building Pattern

Suppose a conjecture concerns a special family of finite structures. The following is a research
pattern, not a claim that this program solves every such conjecture.

Start by studying the larger class generated by natural constructions on the family. Explore
products, restrictions, quotients, or gluing where they have mathematical meaning. Classify basic
examples, prove closure and representation results, and identify where the constructions fail.
Those failures may reveal a more natural ambient class or a missing invariant.

Develop the invariants as a theory: establish transformation laws, investigate which objects
realize a given invariant, and understand how much structure the invariants forget. Connections
with algebra or geometry may then deserve their own foundations and comparison problems. These
parts can be valuable before anyone knows how to express the initial conjecture through them.

Consolidate the constructions, representations, and examples. Only later might their interaction
suggest a structural statement that contains the original family as a special case. If so, prove
that specialization. If not, assess the new mathematical understanding on its own merits and
continue developing its natural questions where justified.

The progress in the middle of this program is the growing body of mathematics and its internal
connections. It cannot always be measured as a shrinking list of obligations for the original
conjecture.

## Contrasting Decisions

- **No known return, substantial foundations:** natural operations, informative examples, and
  representation results are developing coherently. Continue the theory; describe the return to
  the conjecture as unknown.
- **A review arrives before a structural theorem:** definitions and examples have exposed an
  important distinction, but the fundamental laws still need work. Use the review to consolidate
  and choose that work, rather than treating elapsed attempts as a failed proof route.
- **A promising connection needs a second theory:** develop the relevant neighboring structures
  and their comparisons. An immediately importable theorem is not required.
- **Unexpected results reach beyond the conjecture:** a representation theory yields new
  structural theorems for a wider class, with independent applications and natural questions.
  Assess its novelty and propose a standalone paper or a distinct theory branch. This outcome
  may be as important as resolving the initiating conjecture, whose status remains separate.
- **A proposed branch only multiplies cases:** a larger census and finer case splits leave the
  same conjecture to prove separately in each cell. Do not open a branch merely to extend this
  list. Seek a common mechanism or a substantive independent question; a structural
  classification that explains the cases would be a different and potentially valuable outcome.
- **More formalism, no deeper account:** new names merely restate assumptions, constructions lack
  natural examples, and generalizations do not explain anything further. Revise the foundations
  or direction. Breadth alone does not establish value.
- **The user sets a short deadline:** undertake a coherent portion of the theory work and report
  its scope honestly. Do not promise an extensive completed theory or silently exceed the budget.
